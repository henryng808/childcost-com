Screen Smart Children: The Screen-Time Bill Nobody Audits

ChildCost Editorial Team
November 12, 2026 • 8 min read

Screen Smart Children: A guide to helping kids thrive in a world that never switches off by Dr Mark A. Williams and Gavin McCormack is published on November 10, 2026 by Simon & Schuster Australia. It runs 256 pages and lists at $19.00, with an ebook and an unabridged audio edition released the same day. Williams is a cognitive neuroscientist with more than twenty-five years of research behind him, over seventy published papers, three books and positions at institutions including MIT. He was awarded the Medal of the Order of Australia for his contribution to education, lives in Sydney, and describes himself, in the author note, as the Uber driver for his two teenagers. McCormack spent more than twenty-five years in classrooms, including a decade as a trained Montessori teacher, co-founded Upschool.co, has built schools, libraries and teacher-training centres in Nepal, and has been named by The Educator as one of Australia's most influential educators for five consecutive years.

Screen time is the only line in a child's budget that gets cheaper and more expensive in the same year. The hardware costs less than it did a decade ago; the hours cost more. And unlike a swimming lesson or a tutor, it is not a decision a family makes once a term. It is a decision made daily, usually while tired, and it is the one category where the household's own usage sets the benchmark for the child's.

Neither the Ban Nor the Aisle

The publisher's framing is deliberately unhelpful to both camps. This is a guide to raising “capable, compassionate and tech-wise kids in a hyperconnected age”, and it promises a roadmap “without banning screens or battling over devices”. That sentence is the book's commercial risk and its actual value. The abstinence shelf sells certainty in a slim hardback; the permissive shelf sells nothing at all. A book that refuses both positions has to do the harder work of describing what a workable arrangement actually looks like on a Tuesday.

The division of labour follows the authors' backgrounds. Williams supplies the neuroscience: how screen exposure shapes attention, what the evidence supports, and what it does not. McCormack supplies the classroom, where thirty children arrive with thirty different domestic arrangements and no teacher has the authority to fix any of them. The publisher's summary of the payoff is unusually concrete for the category — understand the impact of technology on development, learn to reduce the risks of overexposure, and gain tools that support mental health and wellbeing across the whole family. The last clause is the one that matters. The unit of intervention is the household, not the child, which is both the more honest framing and the more expensive one to accept.

What the Book Actually Asks You to Buy

Almost nothing. The recommendations are behavioural and mostly architectural: where the device lives, when the house is offline, whose phone is face-down at dinner. The Sydney Morning Herald review quoted by the publisher calls it “easy to read and full of useful counsel” that “takes the angst out of helping children get screen smart”. Dr Justin Coulson's line — that the book “doesn't just explain the problem – it hands you the solutions” — is the blurb that matters, because this shelf is long on diagnosis and short on scripts. Angela Falkenberg, president of the Australian Primary Principals Association, frames it as “a practical, research-informed guide to fostering healthy brain development, resilience and meaningful connection”, which is the language schools use when they want parents to stop asking them to solve it.

That is also the book's limitation, stated plainly: it is not a programme, and it will not produce a number. A family looking for a threshold — two hours, ninety minutes, none before three — will not find one defended here, because the authors treat exposure and context as the variables rather than ownership. For a household that wants a rule, this is frustrating. For a household that has already tried the rule and abandoned it in the school holidays, it is the more useful book.

The Line Item Nobody Audits

There is a household finance argument buried in the subject. A family will scrutinise a tutoring invoice and never audit the subscription stack: three streaming services, two game passes, an app that promises better sleep, another that promises better reading, a maths programme bought in September and forgotten by November. Each is small. The aggregate is a second childcare payment, billed monthly, invisible because it arrives in increments.

Screen Smart Children does not price any of this, and it is not a budgeting book. But its central claim — that exposure, not ownership, is the variable — has a direct consequence for spending. The marginal device is rarely the problem; the marginal hour always is. A family that audits hours before it audits hardware will usually find that the most expensive screen in the house is the one belonging to the adult who is too tired to negotiate.

Lessons for Families:

  • Audit the Stack Before the Rules: Add up the subscriptions before writing the screen-time policy. Most families are paying for four things they cannot name and one they cannot cancel.
  • Change the Room, Not the Rule: A device that charges in the kitchen is cheaper than an argument, and it holds up on the days the argument does not.
  • Price Your Own Attention: The benchmark a child learns is the household's usage. The most expensive screen-time intervention is the one the parent will not follow.

The ChildCost Verdict

Screen Smart Children arrives nine days before the holiday hardware season, which is the week the category's spending gets decided in kitchens and delivered in boxes. At $19.00 it costs less than one month of the subscriptions it will make a family question, and it is the rare title on this shelf that argues for fewer purchases rather than a better device. It will not tell you how many hours are acceptable. It will tell you, with a neuroscientist's precision and a teacher's patience, which hours are the ones worth paying to remove — and that is the only version of the question a household can actually budget for.
