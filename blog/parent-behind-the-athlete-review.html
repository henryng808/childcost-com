The Parent Behind the Athlete: The Investment That Doesn't Owe You a Return

ChildCost Editorial Team
November 11, 2026 • 7 min read

The Parent Behind the Athlete: Lessons I Learned the Hard Way About Raising Kids in Sports by BJ Johnson is published on November 17, 2026 by Invisible Scoreboard Publishing. It runs 241 pages, lists at $19.99 in paperback and $9.99 on Kindle, and carries the ISBN 9798998307607. Johnson writes as a father, a coach, a hitting instructor and a mentor — the order matters, because the book is organised around the parent he was rather than the parent he wishes he had been.

Youth sports is one of the few child-rearing categories where the spending is voluntary, escalating and socially enforced at the same time. Nobody is required to enter a travel team. But once a family is three tournaments and two private lesson packages into a season, the decision has already been made by the calendar, and the next invoice is easier to pay than to question. Johnson's subject is the part of that cycle that does not appear on any statement.

The Ledger Nobody Hands You

The book's ten topics read like an audit of the sports parent's year: the invisible scoreboard, burnout, expectations, playing time, recruiting, the investment, identity, communication, failure, letting go. Only one of those is explicitly financial, and it is the one that quietly governs the other nine. Johnson treats “the investment” as a psychological category as much as a budgetary one, which is the honest way to treat it. Families rarely resent the fees. They resent the possibility that the fees bought nothing.

That is where the arithmetic gets dangerous. A parent who has spent six years and a used car on a child's athletic development is not a neutral observer of whether the child should keep playing. The spending creates a stakeholder, and the stakeholder starts making decisions. One advance reader, Cynthia Q., describes the exact mechanism: years of tournaments, lessons and a rearranged family life, and then the recognition that “just because we sacrifice so much doesn't mean our child owes us a certain outcome.” That sentence is the most expensive one in the book, and it is free.

Sunk Costs With a Child's Name on Them

The chapters on expectations, playing time and recruiting are where a household can most easily mistake spending for strategy. Paid coaching cannot buy minutes on a field, and no tournament fee purchases a college roster spot, though the youth sports economy is built on the suggestion that both are downstream of the right expenditure. Johnson's account is corrective rather than reassuring: he describes the mistakes from inside them, including the seasons where his own identity was tangled up in a daughter's performance.

The book is not a cost guide and does not offer a return-on-investment framework. What it offers is earlier warning. A family that notices the sunk-cost reflex in year two can still choose a cheaper league; a family that notices it in year seven is choosing between two versions of the same discomfort.

The Cheapest Intervention in Youth Sports

The strongest material is domestic rather than financial. Johnson's framing of the car ride home — that the minutes after a hard game shape a child more than anything that happened on the field — costs nothing and is, by his own account, the thing he got wrong longest. Another advance reader, Paul R., describes the chapter about a daughter walking away from softball, and the difficulty of accepting that a dream you funded can change without the money being wasted.

That is the book's real argument: the games end, and the relationship does not. Everything a family spends on sport is a bet on the second half of that sentence, and the bet is decided in the car, not at the tournament.

Lessons for Families:

  • Separate the Fee From the Forecast: Pay for the season in front of you, not the scholarship you are imagining. The invoice is real; the projection is not.
  • Watch for the Stakeholder Reflex: The moment you find yourself arguing for more spending because of what you already spent, the child's season has become your sunk cost.
  • Fund the Ride Home: The cheapest part of youth sports is the conversation afterward, and it is the part most likely to be skipped when the schedule is expensive.

The ChildCost Verdict

The Parent Behind the Athlete arrives at the start of the indoor season, which is when travel-team deposits are due and when the year's largest discretionary child expense gets committed. At $19.99 it is roughly the price of one private lesson, and it is the only purchase in the category that might talk a parent out of the next six. It will not tell you what a season should cost. It will tell you, in specific and unflattering detail, what a season costs when the parent's identity is on the roster too — and that is the part of the invoice nobody itemises.
