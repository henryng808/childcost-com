What Should My Children Do?: Buying Capabilities Instead of Credentials

ChildCost Editorial Team
November 11, 2026 • 8 min read

What Should My Children Do?: A Human's Guide to the Age of AI by Daniel Susskind is published in the United States on November 17, 2026 by Penguin Press. It runs 240 pages. The UK edition appeared on September 10, 2026, which is why British reviewers have already had three months with it. Susskind is the Mercers' School Memorial Professor of Business at Gresham College, a digital fellow at the Stanford Digital Economy Lab, a senior research associate at Oxford's Institute for Ethics in AI, and a member of the UK government's Expert Panel on AI and the Future of Work. His previous book, Growth: A Reckoning, was among Barack Obama's favourite books of 2024.

Education is the largest line item in the eighteen-year budget this site exists to calculate, and it is the one most often defended by a single assumption: that the spending buys a credential, and the credential buys a wage. Susskind's book is an examination of what happens to that assumption if the tasks underneath entry-level work are performed by machines. It is the rare parenting title whose subject is not how to raise a child but what the economy will do with one.

The Question Behind the Tuition

The title is deliberately not “what should my children be.” It is a question about activity, and the framing matters for anyone paying school fees. If the answer to “what should they do” is a job title, then the family's plan is a forecast of a labour market fifteen years out, made by the people with the least information and the most anxiety. If the answer is a set of capabilities, the plan is more expensive to describe and cheaper to defend.

Susskind has spent fifteen years on the impact of technology on work, and the publisher describes the result as an evidence-based look at the problem with a practical path forward — for parents, but also for adults whose own jobs are being reorganised at the same time. That dual audience is the book's most useful feature for a household. The parent deciding how much to spend on a child's education is usually also the person whose income funds it, and both decisions now depend on the same forecast.

Optimism Is Not the Same as Reassurance

The Guardian's assessment, quoted by the publisher, is that Susskind “neatly weaves the conceptual and the practical” and is “at once challenging, compelling and refreshingly optimistic.” The optimism is worth naming precisely, because the AI-and-children shelf has produced two lazy genres: the book that says everything will be fine and the book that says everything is already lost. A guide that takes the technology seriously without treating it as an ending is more useful than either, and considerably harder to write.

It is also harder to sell. Anxiety converts well; a measured argument does not. Readers looking for a list of subjects to switch to, or a single skill to force-feed a nine-year-old, will not find one here. What they will find is the reasoning that makes such lists look premature.

What a Parent Can Actually Buy

The honest answer to the cost question is that most education spending is irreversible at the point of purchase. A tutoring package bought in year four cannot be refunded in year nine if the labour market moves. That makes the decision unusually dependent on judgement rather than product choice, which is exactly the terrain Susskind occupies. The book's contribution is not a purchase recommendation but a way of evaluating one: what capability does this spending build, and would it still be worth having if the job at the end of it looked different.

That question is cheap to ask and expensive to skip, and it applies equally to a $40 workbook and a four-year degree. It is also the only question in the category that a family can answer without knowing what the economy will look like in 2040.

Lessons for Families:

  • Name the Capability, Not the Credential: If you cannot say what a purchase builds apart from a qualification, you are buying a forecast rather than an education.
  • Budget for the Parent Too: The income funding a child's education is exposed to the same disruption. Retraining is a family expense, not a personal one.
  • Distrust Both Comforts: “The robots will handle it” and “nothing will change” cost the same amount to believe and are equally unhelpful for planning.

The ChildCost Verdict

What Should My Children Do? is the most expensive kind of parenting book, in the sense that it will not tell you what to buy. It is also the one most likely to change what a family buys, because it moves the question from which credential to which capability, and that shift tends to reduce spending rather than increase it. At 240 pages it is short enough to finish before a school application deadline and serious enough to be worth the interruption. Susskind's earlier book was about whether growth would continue; this one is about what a parent should do while the answer is being decided, which is the only position any household actually occupies.
