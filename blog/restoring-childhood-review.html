Restoring Childhood: The Case Against the Leash

ChildCost Editorial Team
October 27, 2026 • 9 min read

Peter Gray has spent a career arguing one unfashionable thing: children are competent, and much of what we call childhood disorder is the predictable result of denying them the chance to prove it. In Restoring Childhood: How to Set Kids Free in the Age of Anxiety, published in September 2026, the Boston College research professor and author of Free to Learn turns that argument on the current mental health debate — and rejects its most popular explanation.

Not the Phones

Gray's claim is that the rise in childhood anxiety and depression did not begin with smartphones. It began with a decades-long contraction of independence: unsupervised play replaced by scheduled activity, walking replaced by driving, free time replaced by achievement, and a growing assumption that children are fragile unless an adult is watching. The book traces how round-the-clock news coverage, car-dependent neighborhoods, standardized curriculum, and widening inequality each narrowed the territory a child could call their own.

"Restoring Childhood is the book this moment needs," writes Jennifer Breheny Wallace, author of Never Enough. "Peter Gray makes an irrefutable case for letting children be children and lays out a clear path to make it happen." It is a useful corrective to a policy conversation that has settled on restriction as the only available tool.

The Cheapest Line in the Budget

The financial argument is quieter but harder to ignore. Intensive parenting is expensive: the enrichment classes, the supervised hours, the logistics of never letting a child out of sight. Independence costs almost nothing and returns, by Gray's account, confidence, resilience, and the capacity to solve problems without an adult. For families deciding what they can afford, that inversion is worth sitting with — the most protective thing available may also be the least expensive.

Lessons for Families:

  • Audit the Leash: Count how many hours a week your child is unsupervised by any adult. Most families are surprised by how low the number is.
  • Restore Territory: Independence grows with a place to exercise it — a block, a park, a route walked alone.
  • Treat Boredom as Infrastructure: Unstructured time is where children rehearse the self-direction adults later expect of them.

The ChildCost Verdict

Gray writes with the patience of someone who has made this argument for twenty years and expects to keep making it. Restoring Childhood is the most useful book of the season for parents who suspect the current arrangement is both too expensive and not working.
