Ordinary Days and Tender Places: What Adoption Costs Before the Child Arrives

ChildCost Editorial Team
November 12, 2026 • 7 min read

Ordinary Days and Tender Places by Margaret Bendroth is published on November 3, 2026 by Beacon Press. It runs 184 pages and lists at $27.95 in hardcover. Bendroth is a historian and an adoptive parent, and the book is what its publisher calls a blend of personal memoir, history, and broader reflection on American culture — three registers that rarely share a page, and that here are forced to.

Adoption is the only route to parenthood with an itemised bill at the front. Home studies, agency fees, legal work, travel, translation: the costs arrive before the child does, and they arrive in a sequence of invoices that a family pays while being assessed. Every other child expense in this site's arithmetic is spread across eighteen years and can be deferred, renegotiated or refused. This one cannot. That structural difference is why a book about the meaning of adoption belongs in a conversation about its price.

The Bill at the Front

Bendroth is careful not to write a consumer guide, and the book will disappoint anyone hoping for a fee schedule. What it offers instead is the part that a fee schedule cannot answer: why the transaction feels unlike any other, and why the discomfort is not a flaw in the process but a signal about what the process is doing.

Her starting observation is that adoption touches “some of our most vexing social questions, about race and sexuality, abortion rights, economic inequality, even foreign policy and global poverty”, and then “prods us in tender places, reminding us of our own questions about belonging and separation, identity and alienation, our worries about what is determined by fate and what is freely choose.” That list is why the subject attracts bad writing from both directions: the account that treats adoption as charity, and the account that treats it as theft. Bendroth refuses both, which is harder than it sounds and considerably less marketable.

The Normal Family Is the Expensive Story

The book's central move is historical. “Raising children born to someone else is a time-honored human practice,” she writes, and the modern American ideal of the two-parent, biologically related nuclear family is “an oddity” within the long history of humanity. Our ideas about kinship, and our beliefs about what constitutes a normal household, are “surprisingly narrow and, in the end, not all that helpful.”

For a household, the practical consequence of that argument is about the story rather than the money. A family that measures itself against a norm it cannot meet will keep buying things to close the gap — the family-tree project, the heritage test, the second opinion on attachment, the therapy that begins with the premise that something is missing. A family that understands its arrangement as one of many human arrangements spends differently: less on normalisation, more on the ordinary work of raising a person.

Bendroth does not let the reader off with that consolation. She takes seriously “the criticisms and the personal pain many adoptees face”, and the book's second half draws on biology and evolutionary anthropology — findings about kinship practices, genetics and inheritance, even motherhood itself — to show how much of what feels natural is actually recent and local. Rhonda M. Roorda, a Black transracial adoptee, describes feeling “a deep connection to this work”; Barbara Katz Rothman calls it a thoughtful history “with its limitations, problems, and concerns”. A book that earns praise from adoptees and from adoption's critics is doing something more useful than advocacy.

What the Historian Adds to the Ledger

The financial argument is implicit but consistent. Adoption's costs are front-loaded, its risks are distributed unevenly, and its returns are the same as any other childhood's: unpredictable, slow, and not a repayment. A family that has priced the process but not the story will spend the next eighteen years paying for the difference. Bendroth's contribution is to make the story legible before the invoice arrives, which is the only order in which it can help.

Lessons for Families:

  • Budget the Wait, Not Just the Fee: Adoption's costs cluster before placement, which is exactly when a household's income is under the most strain. Plan for the sequence, not the total.
  • Treat “Normal” as a Cost Centre: Every purchase aimed at making a family look ordinary is money not spent on the family it actually is.
  • Ask Who the Book Is Answering: Adoption writing is split between adoptive parents and adoptees. A book that can be read by both is worth the hardcover price.

The ChildCost Verdict

Ordinary Days and Tender Places is short, unsentimental and unusually well positioned: a historian's argument about kinship that doubles as a mirror for the reader's own assumptions about what a family is supposed to cost. At 184 pages it is a single evening, and at $27.95 it is cheaper than most of the normalisation purchases it might talk a family out of. It will not tell you what adoption costs. It will tell you which parts of the cost are the process, and which parts are the story you brought with you — and the second category is the one a household can actually control.
