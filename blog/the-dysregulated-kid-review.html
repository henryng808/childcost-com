The Dysregulated Kid: You Cannot Discipline a Nervous System

ChildCost Editorial Team
November 7, 2026 • 9 min read

Published September 22, 2026 by Page Two, The Dysregulated Kid: The Parenting Playbook for Helping Your Child Find Calm in a Chaotic World by Dr. Roseann Capanna-Hodge opens with three questions that most discipline books answer too quickly. Why does your child lose it over something that seems small? Why don't rewards and consequences work? And why do you feel like you're failing when you are trying so hard? Her answer, drawn from more than thirty years as a therapist and certified school psychologist, is that parents are starting in the wrong place. Behaviour is a signal. The nervous system comes first, and correction comes after.

The 328-page book is built around a method she calls Regulation First Parenting, along with two named tools: the Love Pause, a way for a parent to regulate themselves before responding, and the CALMS Dysregulation Protocol, a five-step framework for deciding what to do in the moment. Whether or not a reader adopts the acronyms, the underlying claim is the one worth testing at home.

The Expensive Cycle

Most families do not arrive at a book like this in a calm season. They arrive after the sticker charts failed, after the consequences escalated, after a preschool or a relative suggested an evaluation. Capanna-Hodge names that cycle precisely: yelling, bribing, punishing, and then the guilt that follows, which resets the whole loop. The reason it is expensive is not metaphorical. Failed behaviour plans turn into assessments, appointments, tutors, lost working days, and the private cost of a parent who is exhausted before breakfast.

Her alternative is co-regulation: the idea that a calm adult nervous system is contagious, and that moving from co-dysregulation to co-regulation changes what happens next. It is a modest-sounding shift with an uncomfortable implication. If the child's state is partly a response to the adult's state, then the parent is not a technician applying a method to a malfunctioning child. The parent is part of the circuit.

What the Book Actually Asks

The playbook asks parents to stop treating the meltdown as the problem to be eliminated and start treating it as information about capacity. That reframing has practical consequences: fewer demands during a dysregulated moment, more attention to the conditions that precede it, and small doable strategies repeated rather than a dramatic overhaul attempted once. The book promises improved focus, independence and resilience, and frames those outcomes as downstream of regulation rather than of stricter enforcement.

Where the book earns its place on a family shelf is in the permission it gives. It tells parents who have been told their child needs more discipline that the evidence points elsewhere, and it gives them language for the school meeting, the family dinner, and the paediatric appointment where that argument has to be made. Language like that saves money. It shortens the distance between a struggling household and the right kind of help.

Lessons for Families:

  • Regulate Before You Correct: A demand delivered to a dysregulated brain is a demand the brain cannot process — wait for capacity, then teach.
  • Use the Love Pause: Regulating yourself first is not indulgence; it is the mechanism by which co-regulation works.
  • Read Behaviour as a Signal: The meltdown is data about load, not evidence that the consequences were too soft.

The ChildCost Verdict

The Dysregulated Kid is a practical book with a clinical spine and a mother's impatience. It will not replace a therapist for a child who needs one, and it does not claim to. What it does is stop families from spending years and thousands of dollars on the wrong intervention first. For parents caught in the yelling-and-guilt loop, that alone is worth the cover price.
