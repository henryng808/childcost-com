You Are the Parent: The Power Struggle You Keep Losing

ChildCost Editorial Team
November 10, 2026 • 7 min read

You Are the Parent: Move from Overparenting to Balanced Parenting and Become the Leader Your Child Needs by Dr. Siggie Cohen was published on September 8, 2026 by Harvest, an imprint of HarperCollins. Cohen is the parenting educator behind the account @dr.siggie, and she brings decades of work in early childhood education and developmental psychology to a book built around a single question the publisher prints on the cover copy: do you feel like you are stuck in a power struggle with your little one, and that he might be winning?

The book's diagnosis of how families arrive there is a list of verbs with the same prefix. Modern parenting, in Cohen's account, has become over-accommodating, over-explaining, over-negotiating, over-emphasizing and over-fixing. The result, she argues, is a generation of children without strong coping skills and a generation of parents trapped in endless tug-of-war for control. Whatever a household spends on parenting — on courses, consultants, calming kits, reward charts, the occasional bribe — most of it is being spent trying to end that tug-of-war rather than prevent it.

Discipline Is Not What You Think It Is

Cohen's framework is the Emotional/Behavioral Proficiency method, or EBP, which she presents as a way to read the root cause of a behaviour before responding to the behaviour itself. The move is familiar to anyone who has read widely in this genre — look past the problem behaviour to the unmet need beneath — but Cohen's version is unusually concrete. The book promises scripts and tools rather than principles alone, aimed at teaching a child the emotional skills behind self-regulation instead of purchasing compliance in the moment.

That distinction is where the money is. Compliance is cheap to buy and expensive to maintain: the sticker chart, the timer, the app, the small reward, repeated until the reward stops working and the negotiation starts again. Skill is expensive to teach and free to keep. A parent who spends six months on scripts has bought something that compounds; a parent who spends six years on incentives has rented calm by the week.

The Scenes You Actually Live In

What makes the book practical is its refusal to aim at an abstract child. The publisher's list of covered ground is a tour of the moments that break a household's evening: tantrums and outbursts, transitions and change, lying and stealing and bragging, and the whining that Cohen writes out phonetically as whyyyyyy. These are not the dramatic failures of parenting literature. They are the recurring costs — the lost hour, the abandoned errand, the dinner that goes cold — that make raising a child feel more expensive than the receipts suggest.

Transitions deserve their own note. Much of what looks like defiance is a child being asked to stop something without warning and start something unexplained, and the resulting collapse is treated by many families as a discipline problem to be solved with consequences. Cohen treats it as a predictable event to be prepared for. Preparation costs a minute. Consequences cost an evening.

The book lands as an audiobook narrated by the author, running roughly ten hours and forty-one minutes, which is a reasonable proxy for how much of this is meant to be absorbed in the car rather than studied at a desk. Cohen's audience built her reputation on short-form advice, and the risk of that format is that it produces recognition without method. The book is an attempt to supply the method.

Lessons for Families:

  • Stop Buying Compliance: Rewards and negotiated settlements rent calm. Skills are the only parenting purchase that does not need renewing.
  • Prepare Transitions, Don't Police Them: Most meltdowns are scheduling problems wearing a discipline costume.
  • Trust Yourself Again: Cohen's stated aim is to restore parental authority without abandoning empathy. A leader who is unsure of their own authority negotiates more, and negotiating more is what created the struggle.

The ChildCost Verdict

You Are the Parent is not a book about spending less, and it does not pretend to be. It is a book about where authority sits in a household, written for parents who have discovered that explaining, accommodating and fixing all cost more than they expected and deliver less than they hoped. Cohen's contribution is a workable script library for the ordinary conflicts that consume a family's evenings — and a reminder that the person who is supposed to be in charge is the one holding the book.
