Connecting: The Screen Rules Were Never the Point

ChildCost Editorial Team
November 7, 2026 • 8 min read

Released in October 2026 by the American Academy of Pediatrics, Connecting: Raising Kids in the Digital World by Megan A. Moreno and Jenny Radesky arrives with a problem the authors name directly on the first page: the market for parental fear is far larger than the market for parental help. Moreno is a professor of pediatrics at the University of Wisconsin and a practicing adolescent medicine physician; Radesky directs developmental behavioral pediatrics at the University of Michigan. They are also co-medical directors of the AAP Center of Excellence on Social Media and Youth Mental Health. Their 240-page book is an attempt to replace hype with the kind of guidance a pediatrician would actually give in an exam room.

That framing matters for anyone budgeting a childhood. Fear is cheap to produce and expensive to act on. Parents who believe screens are a toxin buy filters, timers, subscriptions, surveillance apps and, in the worst cases, residential programs and therapy for a problem that was never diagnosed. A book that starts with evidence rather than alarm is, in household terms, a cheaper book.

Help, Not Hype

The publisher's description of the book is unusually blunt about its target: since The Anxious Generation, concern about children's digital lives has moved "from merely high to nearly frantic," and the authors argue that parents need help, not hype. Their alternative is a framework rooted in the parent-child relationship rather than in device policy. That does not mean the book is permissive. It means the unit of intervention is the relationship and the household's habits, not the tablet.

Moreno and Radesky write as clinicians addressing the concerns that actually bring families into clinic: sleep, mental health, and physical activity. Those three domains are where the pediatric evidence is strongest, and where a family's money is most directly at stake. Sleep loss shows up as behavioral problems that get evaluated, medicated and tutored. Anxiety shows up as school refusal that costs a parent working days. Inactivity shows up in a pediatric appointment and then in a specialist bill. A book that organizes screen advice around health outcomes rather than screen minutes is pointing parents at the part of the ledger that compounds.

The Advice Nobody Sells

The most quietly radical element of Connecting is its instruction to normalize parents' own struggles with media use. Most screen-time products are sold on the premise that the parent is the enforcer and the child is the problem. That model collapses the first time an adult checks a phone at dinner, and children notice hypocrisy long before they obey rules. By treating parental use as part of the same system, the book removes the moral framing that makes household conflict expensive — the arguments, the negotiations, the silent resentment that outlasts any app subscription.

For families comparing the cost of raising a child across eighteen years, this is the honest line item. Devices are cheap; conflict is not. Neither is the substitution of surveillance for conversation, which buys compliance in the short term and charges for it in adolescence, when the child who was monitored rather than trusted stops telling you anything.

Lessons for Families:

  • Start With Sleep, Not Screens: The book's clinical anchor is health — sleep, mental health, activity — not a minute count that no family can sustain.
  • Make Your Own Use Visible: Radesky and Moreno treat parental media habits as part of the family system, not a secret exemption.
  • Buy Tools, Not Verdicts: A filter is a tool. A story about what screens are doing to your child's brain is a bill you keep paying.

The ChildCost Verdict

Connecting will not tell you how many minutes are acceptable, and that will frustrate readers looking for a number. What it offers instead is a defensible framework from two of the pediatricians most responsible for the evidence base — one that treats the family relationship as the asset worth protecting. In a category built on selling anxiety, that is the rarer product.
